The Three-Pass System
01
Before lecture
Pre-read Skim
// 20–35 min · not a full read
  • Read intro + summary first — two minutes max
  • Scan major section headings only
  • Glance at figures at section starts
  • Note unfamiliar bolded terms — don't define them
  • Write 2–3 genuine questions — curiosity gaps
  • For new content: vocab + worked examples only
02
During lecture
Capture
// compressed phrases · your own words
  • Rephrase everything — never copy verbatim
  • Answer "what is this?" + "why does it matter?"
  • Leave ⅓ page as right margin — draw before class
  • Use indentation for hierarchy, not bullets
  • Dash or star when pace picks up — keep moving
  • Formulas get a plain English annotation underneath
03
Within 24 hours
Post-Process
// 15–20 min · highest retention leverage
  • Fill right margin with chapter links + clarifications
  • Write "So What?" sentence per page
  • Fill gaps from textbook — not a re-read
  • Do 1–2 tutorial questions while fresh
  • Flag remaining gaps — your revision hit list

Main body — ⅔ of page

Compressed phrases in your own words. Each point answers "what is this?" or "why does it matter?" Indent sub-points. Formulas always get a plain English annotation. Leave white space between ideas.

Right margin — ⅓ of page (6–7cm on A4)

Left empty during lecture. Filled within 24 hours. Use for chapter links, connections to other weeks, gap clarifications, and tutorial questions. Draw the line before class.

"So What?" box — bottom of each page

One sentence. Written after lecture. Not a definition — an insight or consequence. If you can't write it, the concept isn't solid yet. That's useful to know now, not in the exam.

The "So What?" Sentence

What it is + why it works

One sentence per page, written after the lecture. Forces you to decide what mattered most and state it in plain English without jargon. If you can't compress it, that's a signal the concept isn't fully understood yet.

Cognitively this works because it forces elaborative encoding — connecting information to meaning and consequence rather than just restating it. Over a semester your "So What?" sentences become a powerful rapid-fire revision document.

✕ Bad — definition, not insight
"WACC is the weighted average of the costs of each component of a firm's capital structure adjusted for tax."
✓ Good — consequence + implication
"Using one WACC for every project means you'll fund risky projects that shouldn't proceed and reject safe ones that should."
✓ Good — ECON2271
"OLS finds the line that minimises total squared errors — the squaring means big mistakes are penalised much more than small ones."
✓ Good — ECON2233
"Price floors always create surplus because they prevent the market reaching the price where supply equals demand."

Do / Don't
✓ Do
  • Rephrase everything in your own words — even one word changed means your brain engaged
  • Leave white space between distinct ideas — visual structure, not wasted paper
  • Draw the margin line before class, all pages at once
  • Draw every diagram yourself in ECON2233 — passive slide exposure does almost nothing
  • Use a dash and keep going when pace picks up — a gap beats a pile-up every time
  • Write formulas + plain English annotation immediately underneath
  • Post-process within 2 hours ideally, 24 hours maximum
  • Write "So What?" per page — even rough, the act of writing is the point
  • For ECON2271: redo problems you got wrong rather than re-reading theory
✕ Don't
  • Don't transcribe — hand copying with brain off is wasted time
  • Don't skim from page one progressively — over-cover the start, miss the back half
  • Don't write questions you already know the answer to
  • Don't write "So What?" as a definition — consequences stick, definitions don't
  • Don't chase a missed point while the lecturer moves on — mark it and keep going
  • Don't write full sentences during lecture — compressed phrases are faster and just as useful
  • Don't make the margin too narrow — 6–7cm on A4, drawn deliberately
  • Don't skip post-processing because the lecture felt clear — clarity fades faster than you think
  • For new content: don't try to deeply understand before class — vocab is enough

Semester-Long Revision Strategy
🔁 Weekly Mini-Review Fridays · 30 min
Flip through the week's notes and concept maps. Don't re-read — try to recall key ideas with the page face-down. Anything you can't recall gets flagged. This is active retrieval — the most evidence-backed study method available.
📅 Spaced Repetition Every 2 weeks
Every fortnight spend 20 min per unit going back two weeks. Re-do one tutorial problem from each topic. The goal isn't to re-read — it's to attempt recall and rebuild what's fading before it disappears completely.
🏗️ FINA2222 Concept Chain Map
Keep a running one-page diagram showing how each week's topic connects to prior ones. By Week 8 this becomes a powerful exam summary. Cost of capital → capital structure → payout policy → M&A → governance.
🔢 ECON2271 Problem Bank
Keep a folder of every problem you got wrong or found hard. Don't re-read theory for maths — redo the problems. Return to each hard problem twice before the exam. This is your highest-leverage pre-exam activity.
⏱️ Exam Prep 3 weeks out
Shift from learning to practice. Past papers under timed conditions. Mark your own work harshly. Identify whether errors are conceptual (understanding gap) or mechanical (calculation error) — they need different fixes.
🛡️ Saturday Buffer
In quiet weeks, use Saturdays to get one week ahead on readings — this is how you build the slack you need. In heavy weeks (assignments, tests), the buffer absorbs the load without derailing your weekly rhythm.

End-of-Week Habit That Compounds
Every Friday, write a half-page plain-English concept map per unit. No jargon. If you can explain WACC to someone who's never studied finance, you know it. If you can't, that's your revision flag. Over a semester these maps become one of the most efficient revision tools you'll have.
